Conflation — why we can't just multiply the sliders together

Every slider on the calculator is backed by a number from a published study — a hazard ratio (HR): how much one lifestyle trait is associated with the risk of dying, compared to not having that trait. Move several sliders at once and, intuitively, the effects should add up. They do — but only when the traits are truly independent. And a lot of these sliders are secretly measuring the same thing.

Take the movement sliders. Cardio, steps and sitting each come from their own study comparing people who do more against people who do less. But all three are really asking "how physically active are you?" Multiply the three study numbers together and you count the same activity roughly three times:

This is conflation: inputs that share a pathway or overlap being priced as if they were independent. It sneaks in three ways:

  • One trait, several sliders. Fiber, fruit & veg, nuts and fish are four sliders but roughly one "diet quality" trait. Each study hands you the whole benefit of eating well — multiply them and you've charged the same benefit four times.
  • Shared pathway. Exercise lowers body weight; the weight slider then charges the weight part of the exercise benefit a second time.
  • Overlapping studies. Studies adjust for each other's variables, but never perfectly, so a little of each factor is already hiding inside the others' numbers.

This matters. Multiplying blindly, a merely-average healthy lifestyle used to come out as a 93% lower mortality risk — statistically absurd. Even the standard method for combining risk factors (the comparative-risk approach used by the WHO and the Global Burden of Disease project) is only valid when the factors are weakly correlated and do not share pathways (Ezzati 2003). That caveat is the conflation problem.

How the combination math works

The calculator's Advanced mode (the default) combines inputs with three mechanisms, then applies guardrails. Each mechanism below comes with a live worked example, computed from the same model the calculator uses — change the model and these numbers change with it.

1 · Hazard ratios, and why they multiply

An HR is a risk ratio: HR 0.7 means "30% lower risk of dying in a given period", HR 1.3 means "30% higher". If two risks were truly independent, the combined risk is their product — 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.81. That works because on the log scale, multiplication becomes addition: ln(0.9) + ln(0.9) = ln(0.81).

The product rule itself is fine. The problem is its one assumption — independence — and lifestyle traits are not independent. That's what the next steps fix.

2 · Why the naive product overclaims

Each study's HR is a marginal effect: it attributes the whole association to one trait, comparing "high" vs "low" without fully separating the other traits. When the traits overlap, multiplying the marginals charges the shared part again and again — eight diet sliders become one benefit counted eight times.

3 · Fix 1 — joint models: one published study, one number

Where a study publishes a joint estimate — several factors combined in one model — we use it directly and stop multiplying those sliders individually. The sliders become shares of one estimate, not independent effects.

The movement cluster is the cleanest example. Cardio, steps and sitting are priced by one published PA × sitting table (Ekelund 2016):

Same inputs, two answers — the difference is the "physical activity" the naive product charged three times. The joint number is the honest one.

4 · Fix 2 — overlap discounts (ρ) for the leftovers

Not every overlapping pair has a joint study. For those, we discount the weaker of the two effects by ρ — our estimate of how much the pair overlaps. ρ = 0 means independent (no discount); ρ = 1 means the two are the same thing (the weaker effect counts for nothing).

Technically: the smaller same-direction deviation from the average level is multiplied by (1 − ρ) in log space. For the non-math inclined: if two things are half the same thing, the smaller one counts at half strength.

Magnesium is a good example — its food sources are the healthy-diet foods, so it overlaps the diet score heavily (ρ 0.5):

Honesty note: ρ is a model parameter, not a published number. Its plausible range is reflected in the uncertainty band (step 6).

5 · Fix 3 — no combination: shown per lever only

For the psychosocial factors — purpose in life, stress, social connection, sleep regularity — there is no published joint model, and their pairwise correlations are so tangled (people with purpose tend to have better social lives, less stress…) that even pairwise discounting can't be trusted. So we don't combine them at all: each is shown individually, and only their weak, correlational wellbeing points feed the happiness & cognition bands. None enter the mortality, cancer or CVD totals.

6 · After combining — the guardrails

  • Average-person anchor. Everything is normalised so 1.0× = the average person. Reset the calculator and you're exactly average.
  • Clamp. The combined HR is clamped to 0.45–4.0 so no combination can claim more than the studies support.
  • Honest uncertainty. Each effect's confidence interval is widened where evidence is thin and combined in quadrature — you get a range, not a false-precision point. ρ's uncertainty widens the band too.
  • HR → years. Life expectancy is a sex-specific US baseline (NCHS 2023) shifted by your combined risk via a Gompertz approximation (adult risk doubles every ~7 years).

7 · Where multiplying is fair — and where it isn't

Combining HRs under an independence assumption is a published WHO/GBD-family method — valid when the factors are weakly correlated and do not share pathways (Ezzati 2003). We hold every combination we make against this boundary:
