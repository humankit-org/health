How the estimates work (and why you shouldn't fully trust them)

The mortality model

  • Each input maps to a hazard ratio (HR) from a published study — e.g. meeting exercise guidelines: HR 0.69, a 31% lower mortality hazard. Every HR on the calculator page links back to its source in the list below.
  • HRs combine multiplicatively, assuming independence. Real lifestyle effects overlap (exercisers also eat better, sleep better…), so this overstates combined benefits. We therefore cap the total at a 55% hazard reduction (and a 4× increase) — the "capped" note appears when that bites.
  • HR → years of life expectancy uses a Gompertz approximation (adult mortality risk doubles ~every 7 years). Cross-checked against two published results: our exercise HRs reproduce Moore 2012's measured +4.5 y, and the smoking HR reproduces Jha 2013's ">10 years lost".
  • Everything is anchored to the average person. Defaults are population averages (mostly US figures, noted per input), and the engine normalizes against that average profile: 1.0× = the average person, and the average person gets exactly the baseline life expectancy (US 2023, NCHS, by sex). This fixes a subtle incoherence: the studies' reference strata (no exercise, no fiber, never-smoker — a "monk profile") are not the average, so we never mix the two anchors on screen.
  • "Plausible ranges" combine each study's published 95% CI in quadrature (log-space, independent errors) — the same independence assumption as the central estimate. They capture statistical uncertainty of the inputs, not the much larger uncertainty about you.
  • The weaker the evidence, the wider the range. A published CI only captures a study's sampling error — not confounding, not extrapolation, not our approximations. So each effect's CI is widened around its central estimate before combining: ×1 (high evidence), ×1.5 (moderate), ×2.25 (low). Low-evidence ranges will often cross 1.0 — that's the point: we genuinely don't know, and the UI should say so.
  • The central estimate is clamped (to 0.45–4.0×) and the uncertainty range is then applied around the clamped value.
  • Measured values beat reported habits. If you enable a VO2 max value, it replaces the cardio-mortality estimate (fitness predicts mortality better than self-reported activity, and using both would double-count). A measured body-fat % likewise replaces the BMI estimate.
  • The cancer output only sums what's sourced. It combines cancer-specific effect sizes for the inputs that have them (smoking, cardio, processed meat, sugary drinks, sitting, snus, fiber, coffee, vitamin D, fish, fruit & veg) and lists everything else as "no data yet" rather than guessing. It overlaps with all-cause mortality (cancer is roughly a fifth of it) — the two cards are not independent.
  • The CVD output follows the same pattern. It combines cardiovascular-specific effect sizes (~22 inputs with data available from the same primary sources). Like the cancer card, it is transparent about what it doesn't know — inputs without CVD-specific data are listed below the gauge.

Cognition & happiness

There is no honest way to put a number on these. Effects are small, mostly correlational, and often domain-specific (e.g. creatine helps short-term memory and reasoning, mainly in vegetarians and older adults). So we show qualitative bands with a deliberately fuzzy marker — and the marker gets fuzzier for every low-evidence input currently contributing. Nothing here should be read as "your brain score".

The findings card

Below the four metrics, a reactive list shows sourced facts that don't fit on a slider — disease-specific outcomes (lung cancer, heart failure, fractures), honest nulls (vitamin D supplementation's empty RCT result), and caveats. Items appear only when they apply to the current inputs, each with its citation.

What the evidence badges mean

  • high Large, consistent meta-analyses or pooled cohorts with dose–response. Still almost entirely observational — association, not proven causation.
  • moderate Meta-analytic but heterogeneous, small trials, or numbers converted/approximated from the published form (flagged in the source note).
  • low Single cohorts, cross-sectional snapshots, or indirect citations. Directionally interesting, nothing more.

Known limitations

  • Self-reported inputs in the underlying studies; residual confounding everywhere (wealth, education, genetics).
  • US-centric baseline; no age, medical history, or genetics inputs.
  • Some effects are J/U-shaped; we model the published inflection points only roughly.
  • A few contributions reuse a mortality-focused citation for a mind outcome — those are explicitly marked "indirect citation — replace with a dedicated source" in the data file.

Privacy, by design

This is a static site. Your inputs live only in the calculator tab's memory and vanish when you close it. No cookies, no localStorage, no analytics, no backend — and there never will be a database for health inputs on this site.
